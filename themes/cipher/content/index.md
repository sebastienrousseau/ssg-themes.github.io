faq1_q: "Is Cipher a password manager?"
faq1_a: "No. Cipher makes secrets; it does not keep them. There is no vault, no sync and no autofill. Once you copy a passphrase, store it wherever you already keep passwords, or write it on paper and keep the paper somewhere sensible. Cipher forgets it as soon as you close the window."
faq2_q: "Why does Cipher not force a symbol or a digit?"
faq2_a: 'Because a forced rule removes options instead of adding them. Our <a href="https://example.com/cipher/guides/length-first" target="_blank" rel="external noopener noreferrer">length-first guide</a> walks through the sums: a random pick from a bigger pool, or a longer result, adds bits; a rule that every password must end with a digit does not. If a website insists on a symbol, tick the box and Cipher will include the set, and the readout will show what changed.'
faq3_q: "Does anything I generate leave my device?"
faq3_a: "No. The page and the app draw from the random source built into your system (<code>window.crypto.getRandomValues</code> in the browser, the operating system source in the <code>crypto</code> layer of the command-line tool). There is no analytics script and no account, and the word list ships inside the app, so even the words never come over the network."
faq4_q: "Should I pick words or characters?"
faq4_a: 'Pick words for anything you type by hand: a laptop login, a vault master password, the Wi-Fi at home. Pick characters for anything a machine stores. <a href="https://example.com/cipher/word-lists" target="_blank" rel="external noopener noreferrer">The Cipher word list</a> has 7,776 entries, so five words give about 65 bits and six give about 78. An attacker who knows the list still has to try every combination, which is why the count is honest even though the words are ordinary.'
faq5_q: "Who is the decoy set for?"
faq5_a: 'People who build or run a login system. The idea (<a href="https://example.com/cipher/guides/decoy-sets" target="_blank" rel="external noopener noreferrer">our guide has the details</a>) is to store <code>k - 1</code> decoys beside each real password, so a copied list holds <code>k</code> entries that look alike. If someone later signs in with a decoy, your system knows the list has leaked and can lock the account. Cipher produces the set; detecting and alerting is up to your own service, and it only helps if that service is built for it.'
faq6_q: "What does the entropy number actually mean?"
faq6_a: 'It is the number of yes-or-no questions needed to pin down one result, given how Cipher chose it. <a href="https://example.com/cipher/guides/entropy" target="_blank" rel="external noopener noreferrer">For a uniform pick</a> the sum is <code class="math-expr">H = n × log₂(pool size)</code>: six words from 7,776 give 77.5 bits. It describes the method, not your habits, so it only holds if you keep the result exactly as Cipher gave it.'

Cipher is a small passphrase generator made by a team of four in Bristol. It
picks words from a 7,776-word Diceware list, or characters from the keyboard
set, and shows the entropy of every setting as a plain number of bits.

We built it after watching friends and clients reuse one "strong" password for
years because inventing a new one felt like a chore. Six random words take a
second to make, a minute to learn, and carry about 77.5 bits; Cipher shows
that sum next to the result so the choice is yours to make with the facts.

The desktop app, the command-line tool and this web console share one word
list and one counting rule. None of them needs an account, and none of them
sends what you generate anywhere.
